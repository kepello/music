_For Words to Be Written_

More than thirty years ago, I sat down to write a poem. My goal, as I remember it, was something in the style of Edgar Allan Poe—dark, introspective, probably a little dramatic. I don’t remember the poem. I don’t remember any of the other lines.

I remember one:

For words to be written must first be defined,
And this, in truth, is a fear of mine.

And that’s where I stopped. Because as soon as I wrote it, I realized it was true. There were things inside me that I thought probably needed to come out. Things I suspected might even hurt less if I could let them out. But writing them meant defining them.

Defining them meant looking at them clearly enough to give them names. And at that point in my life, I wasn’t ready to do that. So I didn’t finish the poem. More than thirty years passed, then I started writing songs. I wrote about my life. My childhood. My parents. My faith. Sometimes deliberately, and sometimes discovering only afterward that I’d pulled on a thread connected to something much older.

Little by little, those threads began to loosen some things that had been bound up and hurting for a very long time. And songwriting gave me a way to let them out that felt healthy. I could approach something through a story, an image, a rhyme or a melody. I didn’t have to sit down and announce that today I was going to confront something I’d avoided for decades.

I could just write a song, then one day I realized something. The words had been written. Not all of them. There are still things I don’t understand and probably things I haven’t found words for yet. But many of the words I had been afraid to define thirty years ago were now sitting in front of me—in songs.

There was real relief in realizing that. And I decided I was going to take a little credit for it. It took me more than thirty years. That’s a long time to finish a line, but I found a way.

So I went back to the sentence I had started all those years ago:

For words to be written
Must first be defined.

This time, I didn’t stop.

Today I find
I’m finishing that line.

This song isn’t a declaration that everything difficult is behind me. The outro deliberately leaves room for what remains:

Some lines are behind me
And some I still don’t understand.

The difference is that the page isn’t empty anymore. The pen is still in my hand. And after more than thirty years, I think that’s worth taking some credit for.

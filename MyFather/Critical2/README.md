_Critical (2)_

I didn’t return to “Critical” immediately after making the first arrangement, but the song stayed with me. For weeks I found A’shon’s words coming back into my mind, which I suppose is one of the best measures I have for whether a song has really affected me. The more I thought about it, the more I felt that what he had written could resonate with almost anyone who has their eyes wide open to the times we’re living in, but it is especially poignant for someone who reads the Bible and recognizes just how closely the conditions around us resemble what Paul described so long ago.

The first arrangement had also given me something I hadn’t expected. Working with A’shon’s poem was my introduction to Neo Soul, and discovering that music was a wonderful gift that came out of arranging his words. I enjoyed it enough that I later began using elements of it in some of my own music. When I eventually came back to “Critical,” that experience made me wonder what else might be hiding in these lyrics musically. If a style I hadn’t previously explored could fit them so naturally, perhaps there were other ways of hearing the same words that I hadn’t considered.

I had been experimenting with the trippy, psychedelic music of the 1960s, and almost on a whim I wondered what would happen if I put “Critical” there. On paper I’m not sure I would have predicted that combination working, but to me it did, and surprisingly well. Maybe there is something inherent in that musical form that suits the words, or maybe it has something to do with the history of that era and music made by people who were looking around at the world, questioning what they saw and feeling that something was deeply wrong. I’m really not sure. I just know that when I heard A’shon’s words in that setting, the music seemed to match the lyrics beautifully.

What I particularly like is that the arrangement changes the feeling without changing the message. The same images are still there—the ICU, the flatline, the diagnosis, a world in critical condition—but heard through this strange, swirling musical landscape they take on a different character. It feels less like being given a list of symptoms and more like standing in the middle of them, looking around and realizing that what Paul described isn’t difficult to find.

That’s probably why I keep returning to this poem. A’shon took a passage I already knew well and found imagery that made me look at it again. The first arrangement introduced me to a musical language I hadn’t really explored before, and this one showed me that the same words could survive being viewed from a completely different musical angle and somehow reveal something new.

The situation is still Critical. I just found another way to hear it.

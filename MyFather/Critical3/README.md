_Critical (3)_

I’m not completely sure how to explain this version of “Critical.” Like discovering a new ingredient or a kind of food I’d never tried before, I couldn’t seem to stop thinking about A’shon’s poem after arranging it. I had already returned to it once because I wanted to hear what would happen if I moved it away from Neo Soul and into the psychedelic sounds of the 1960s, and by this point I think I was simply having fun with the experience. The words had enough depth that changing the music kept making me hear different things in them, so I wanted to see what else they could survive.

This one, though, started as a joke. I had done something similar with one of my own songs, deliberately putting it into a musical setting that seemed completely wrong, only to discover that I liked the result much more than I expected. So what could possibly be the opposite of the Neo Soul arrangement A’shon originally asked me for? Surely Appalachian mountain bluegrass, complete with upright bass and mandolin, couldn’t possibly work. And while we’re already doing something ridiculous, why not somehow fuse that with rap? That ought to make certain it doesn’t work.

I laughed hard enough making it, and probably even harder sending it to A’shon. As expected, I was right about at least one thing: this was definitely not his interpretation of his music. But then I played it for another friend, and he made an observation I hadn’t expected. Of all the versions, this was the one he liked best. It wasn’t particularly because of the bluegrass music, although he thought that was fine. It was because in this arrangement he could suddenly hear the lyrics clearly. The rap-like delivery brought the words forward in a way the other arrangements hadn’t for him, and he began noticing clever lines, wordplay and subtle connections that he had missed before. He was hearing and appreciating the same things in A’shon’s writing that had made me want to keep returning to the poem in the first place.

That completely changed the way I thought about what had started as a joke. This arrangement isn’t really my kind of music, and it certainly isn’t A’shon’s. I don’t expect either of us to suddenly start making Appalachian bluegrass rap. But music is ultimately there to carry the words, and if this strange combination allows someone to hear those words more clearly, to catch something they missed before, and to appreciate a little more of the craft and talent A’shon put into “Critical,” then I can’t really dismiss it just because it started out making me laugh.

So it stays.

Sometimes apparently the wrong music can make you hear the right words.
